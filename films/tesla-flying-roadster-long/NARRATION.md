[open] This is a car. And it's about to drive upside down.

[title] What if your car could fly? Tesla says this one can.

[when] October fifteenth, twenty twenty-six. A rocket test site in the middle of Texas. Tesla rolls out the new Roadster, and does something no road car has done in public.

[did] It drives up a ramp that curls right over. Hangs off the ceiling, upside down. Then lets go, flips over in mid-air, and lands on its wheels.

[claims] Tesla's claims are wild. Zero to sixty in under one second. Ten rocket-style thrusters. Fans that suck it to the road with two g's of force. On sale in twenty twenty-eight.

[questions] So let's do what the launch video didn't. How does a car hold onto a ceiling? Why can't a plane do the same thing? And can this thing actually fly?

[three] To beat gravity, you need a force pushing up. And there are really only three ways to get one.

[wingIntro] The first is a wing.

[lift] A wing is tilted, just slightly, into the oncoming air. As it moves, it throws that air downward. Push air down, and the air pushes the wing up. That's lift.

[speed] And lift grows fast with speed. Twice as fast gives you four times the lift.

[inverted] Here's the thing people get wrong. Planes can fly upside down. Stunt pilots do it all the time.

[nose] Upside down, the pilot just tips the nose up a little, so the wing still meets the air at an angle, and still throws it downward.

[slow] But watch what happens when the plane slows down. To keep the same lift, the pilot has to tip the wing further and further.

[stall] Until the air can't follow the wing any more. It tears away. That's a stall, and the lift collapses.

[hang] So a wing can never just hang there. It needs speed, all the time.

[downforce] Race cars use the same trick, upside down. Their wings throw air up, so the air pushes the car down. Engineers like to say a Formula One car makes so much downforce, it could drive on a ceiling at racing speed. In theory. Nobody's tried it.

[catch] But there's that word again. Speed. Slow down, and the grip disappears.

[loop] And Tesla's ramp has a speed trick of its own. Think of a toy car on a loop.

[curve] Going round a curve, a car keeps trying to carry on straight. At the top of the loop, straight on means up, into the track. If it's fast enough, that alone pins it to the ceiling.

[numbers] For a loop this size, you'd only need about twenty-five kilometres an hour. Stunt drivers have done full loops in real cars this way.

[flat] But Tesla's ramp doesn't stop at the top. It carries on as a flat ceiling. And a flat ceiling doesn't curve. Nothing pushes you into it. Slow down up there, and you'd fall.

[second] That's where the second way comes in. Suction.

[fans] Under the car there are fans. They pull the air out from underneath, faster than it can get back in.

[pressure] Now the air under the car is thinner than the air all around it. And the air around it pushes harder. From above, from below, from every side. Here, the push that wins is the one pressing the car into the ceiling.

[twog] Tesla says the fans pull with up to two g's. Twice the car's own weight.

[tenth] That sounds like a lot. But spread across the floor of a car, it's a difference of less than a tenth of normal air pressure. The air is already pressing that hard on everything. The fans just take a little away from one side.

[old] And it isn't new. In nineteen seventy, the Chaparral two-J raced with two fans at the back, sucking it to the track. It was banned.

[brabham] In nineteen seventy-eight, Brabham tried a fan car in Formula One. It won its first race. Then it was withdrawn.

[mcmurtry] And in twenty twenty-five, a tiny British electric car called the McMurtry Spéirling used its fans to drive upside down. Fans grip even when you're standing still. No speed needed.

[surface] But suction has one weakness. It needs a surface. The fans can only pull you towards something.

[leave] Leave the ramp, and there's nothing to pull against.

[third] And that's the third way. Thrust.

[newton] Throw something one way, and you get pushed the other. That's all a rocket is.

[cold] The Roadster's thrusters don't burn anything. They're cold-gas thrusters. Air, squeezed into tanks at enormous pressure, and let out through nozzles. Spacecraft use the same kind of thruster to steer.

[nozzles] Tesla says there are ten of them, fed by about six hundred litres of compressed air. Four at the back, one on each corner, two on the front fenders.

[roll] Fire the jets on one side, and the car rolls. Fire them the other way, and the roll stops. That's how it turned upright in mid-air.

[brake] And just before landing, a burst from all four corners slows the fall.

[hover] So here's the big question. If it has thrusters, could it just hover?

[weight] To hold up a car of almost two tonnes, the jets have to push with the car's whole weight. Every second.

[kilos] Cold air coming out of a nozzle is fast, but not that fast. To push that hard, you'd be throwing out roughly thirty kilos of air, every second.

[tanks] And six hundred litres, even squeezed as hard as these tanks allow, holds a few hundred kilos at most.

[seconds] Do the maths, and you get seconds of hovering. Not minutes. Seconds.

[heli] A helicopter can hover all day. It doesn't carry the air it throws down. It grabs it from all around, and its engine keeps going as long as there's fuel.

[bottle] The Roadster carries all of its air in a bottle. When the bottle's empty, the show's over.

[hop] So it doesn't fly like a plane. And it doesn't hover like a helicopter. It hops. Short, sharp bursts. A launch, a jump, a flip.

[kick] Like this.

[check] So, a reality check. Here's what Tesla actually showed.

[shown] A car that clings to a ramp upside down. That jumps and flips on air thrusters. On a closed test site. And, from what was reported, with nobody inside.

[promise] And here's what's still a promise. The one-second launch. The road-going version. The price. And that twenty twenty-eight date. This Roadster was first announced in twenty seventeen, for twenty twenty.

[legal] The flying part also isn't legal on public roads. America's aviation regulator says it isn't an aircraft. So, for now, it's a car that does tricks.

[human] And there's one more question. You.

[blood] Upside down, held up by fans, your seat isn't holding you any more. Your harness is. Your blood heads for your head. And the only thing between you and the ground is air pressure, and a few fans.

[end] So… would you ride in it upside down?
