[open] Ten years from now, you'll be a billionaire. / And you won't stop for her. / You won't even see her.

[title] Would you actually be generous if you suddenly became a billionaire?

[flat] Saturday night. Your flat. / Rent is due on Monday. You have $412.

[draw] The biggest jackpot in history. / You bought one ticket.

[balls] First number. Yours. / Second. Third. Fourth. Fifth. / And the last one.

[won] After tax, it's all yours: / one billion dollars.

[scale] Counting it, one dollar a second, would take almost 32 years. / In $100 bills, it would fill this room to your chest. / And weigh 10 tonnes.

[promise] Everyone knows what they'd do with it. / So you write it down. / Half goes to people who need it.

[mum] Day one. You pay off your mum's house. / Then your best friend's debts. / It feels incredible.

[dunn] That feeling is real, though smaller than first thought. / People handed $5 or $20 to spend on others / ended the day happier than those who spent it on themselves.

[out] Day three. Your name gets out. / 40 messages. / 900. / 2,600.

[who] Cousins you've never met. / Strangers with heartbreaking stories. / You can't tell which ones are true.

[trust] So you stop answering. / And you start to wonder who likes you… / …and who likes your money.

[interest] Meanwhile, your money is making money. / $137,000 a day. In your sleep. / Since this sentence appeared, you've made $5.

[house] Month two. The house. / Eleven bathrooms. You've used two. / And the car.

[neighbours] Back on your old street, a neighbour buys a new car. / In Canada, the bigger someone's lottery win, / the more of their neighbours went bankrupt in the next two years. / Everyone's trying to keep up.

[six] Month six. Breakfast in your huge kitchen. / It doesn't taste special any more.

[brick] In 1978, lottery winners rated everyday pleasures: / breakfast, a joke, a compliment. / They enjoyed them less than people who hadn't won. / To be fair: big lottery winners in Sweden / were still more satisfied with life 5 to 22 years later.

[enough] So you want more. / Researchers asked 2,000 millionaires how much more would make them perfectly happy. / Of those not already very happy, three in four said 2 to 10 times more.

[compare] Would you rather earn $50,000 while everyone else earns $25,000… / …or $100,000 while everyone else earns $200,000? / Asked at Harvard, about half chose to be poorer, as long as they were ahead.


[boss] Year one. You start a company. / Now nobody tells you no. / Everyone laughs at your jokes.

[cookies] In one experiment, three strangers worked at a table. / One was put in charge, at random. / Then someone brought in five cookies.

[cookie2] The one in charge was more likely to take the extra cookie… / …and eat it with their mouth open.

[game] At Berkeley, pairs of strangers played a rigged game of Monopoly. / A coin flip made one of them rich: / double money, double pay, two dice instead of one.

[game2] The rich players got louder. / They ate more of the pretzels. / And when they won, they said it was skill.

[genius] A business magazine puts you on its cover. / You won a lottery. / But you start to believe you earned it.

[views] Even your opinions change. / In Britain, lottery winners drifted right. The bigger the win, the bigger the shift.

[yacht] Year two. Your yacht is 60 metres long. / The one next to it is 120.

[wall] Then a wall. A gate. A guard. / Now people need a reason to reach you.

[walk] Year three. A walk downtown.

[glass] In New York, 61 people wore camera glasses for one block. / The higher their class, the shorter they looked at the people they passed.

[glass2] Shop windows. Your phone. / Barely them.

[drive] Mostly, you drive. / At a Berkeley crossing, drivers of the most expensive cars / didn't stop for pedestrians 46% of the time. / In the cheapest cars, every driver stopped.

[nostop] You don't stop. / You tell yourself you didn't see her.

[years] Year five. Year eight. / You do give. $10 million here, $5 million there. / But your money grows faster than you give it away.

[note] Then you find the note. / Half would be $627 million now.

[pledge] You're not the only one. / In 2010, 57 American billionaires promised to give at least half away. / 15 years later, the 32 still billionaires are 283% richer. / Most of what they gave went to private foundations, often their own. / Of the 22 pledgers who have died, only 8 gave half away.

[real] Is any of this real? / Your story is made up. The studies are real. / Some were small. Some failed when repeated, like the crossing study.

[letters] In a Dutch city, researchers delivered letters with money inside to the wrong homes on purpose. / Rich homes sent back 81%. Poor homes, 38%. / But just after payday, poor homes sent back almost as many. / Big surveys agree: richer people give more dollars. / Whether they give a bigger share is still argued over.

[scott] And one real woman's story began a lot like yours. / In 2019, a divorce suddenly made MacKenzie Scott worth about $36 billion. / Since then, she has given away $26 billion, with no strings attached.

[feeney] Chuck Feeney gave away about $8 billion, mostly in secret. / He kept about $2 million, rented a flat, and wore a cheap watch.

[point] So money may not decide who you become. / But it can make it easier not to notice.

[again] Ten years from now. The same crossing. / This time, you stop. / This time, you see her.

[question] So, be honest. Would you give half away?
