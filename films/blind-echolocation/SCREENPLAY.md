# What If You Were Blind? And Learned to See With Sound

The approved screenplay, as the owner sent it (2026-10-12). The exact narration is in `NARRATION.md` (and was recorded
from it); the times below are the screenplay's approximate targets. The film is timed from the real recordings instead:
see `TIMELINE.md` (the finished film runs 8:23 because the recorded narration is longer than the 7-minute estimate, and
the voice is never cut or sped up).

Duration: approximately 7:00 · Format: 16:9 cinematic animated documentary · Narration: curious, immersive, natural ·
Creative rule: sound visualizations represent spatial information, not literal images that blind people see.

## Scene 1 — The last thing you see (0:00–0:30, hook)

VISUAL. First-person view. A beautifully lit bedroom. Morning sunlight illuminates the walls. You reach toward your
ringing phone. Suddenly, every light in the world vanishes. The screen becomes almost completely black. But the camera
is still moving. The alarm rings. A vehicle passes outside. A quiet footstep comes from behind the door. Each sound
produces a brief, abstract ripple in the darkness.

NARRATOR. "Imagine waking up tomorrow… and discovering you can't see anything. No sunlight. No colors. Not even the shape
of your own hands. But here's the strange part. Your room hasn't disappeared. You can still hear the alarm beside you.
Cars outside. Someone walking down the hallway. And your brain is about to discover that sound can reveal things your
eyes no longer can."

NO NARRATION — 5 seconds. The phone falls from the bed. We hear it bounce, then slide underneath a cabinet. The camera
turns toward the sound. A faint pulse suggests its direction. No music for the first impact. Let the sound feel
uncomfortably close.

## Scene 2 — A world without light (0:30–0:58, the problem)

VISUAL. We briefly see the bedroom from a third-person cinematic perspective. The protagonist is sitting on the bed,
reaching cautiously through the air. Cut into their experience: darkness, with sounds represented by subtle spatial
distortions. The refrigerator hums. A clock ticks. Footsteps pass outside. A faint map of nearby sound sources begins to
appear, but it cannot reveal the entire room.

NARRATOR. "At first, everything takes longer. Finding your clothes. Pouring water. Reaching for a door handle. Things you
once did without thinking suddenly require attention. And contrary to what movies suggest, your other senses don't
instantly become superhuman. But you can learn to pay attention to information that was always there. And one
particular sound is about to change how you understand space."

VISUAL-ONLY ENDING. The character accidentally taps the wall. A sharp sound reflects through the room. The camera holds
on their reaction.

## Scene 3 — The first echo (0:58–1:30, discovery)

VISUAL. The protagonist stands in a hallway. They tap their fingers together. A cinematic sound wave travels outward,
bounces against the far wall and returns. Switch between three perspectives: realistic third-person hallway;
first-person darkness with abstract returning echoes; a transparent 3D cutaway showing sound reflections. Now a doorway
opens. The echo pattern changes.

NARRATOR. "Listen. Every time you make a sound, part of it travels outward. When those sound waves encounter a surface,
some of their energy reflects back toward you. A wall. A doorway. A large piece of furniture. Each changes the sound in
a different way. And if you learn to recognize those changes, echoes can become clues about the space around you.
There's a name for this. Echolocation."

NO NARRATION — 6 seconds. The character makes another click. The sound expands. The camera tracks the wave through the
open doorway into the next room. The reflected pattern changes dramatically.

## Scene 4 — How a human can echolocate (1:30–2:00, the science becomes visual)

VISUAL. Extreme close-up of a person's mouth producing a short, sharp tongue click. Time slows. Thousands of tiny
particles visualize a sound wave traveling through a dark environment. The wave reaches a wall two meters away. It
reflects back. A precise counter appears: 2 METERS → APPROXIMATELY 12 MILLISECONDS ROUND TRIP.

NARRATOR. "Some human echolocators produce sharp clicks using their tongue. Sound travels through air at roughly three
hundred and forty-three meters per second. If a wall is two meters away, the sound can travel there and back in about
twelve milliseconds. That's incredibly fast. Your brain doesn't need to hear a completely separate echo each time. Small
differences in timing, loudness and sound quality can provide useful spatial information."

NO NARRATION — 5 seconds. Three clicks. Three different surfaces. Each produces a different abstract echo visualization.
Let the audience hear the differences.

## Scene 5 — Building a world from sound (2:00–2:33, the first incredible visual reveal)

VISUAL. Weeks pass in a fast, cinematic training montage. The protagonist practices in safe, supervised environments,
initially using their hands and mobility cane for orientation. At first, a click produces only chaotic, scattered
particles. With practice, their perception of obstacles becomes more useful. A large wall is represented as a broad
shimmering plane. An open doorway becomes a gap. A freestanding column appears as a narrow shape. The 3D environment
transitions between ordinary reality and an artistic representation of acoustic space.

NARRATOR. "This is not something you master overnight. But with practice, people can learn to distinguish openings,
nearby obstacles, and even differences between certain shapes. Researchers have trained blind and sighted adults to
echolocate over a period of ten weeks. And both groups improved. Now imagine what that learning process might feel like.
A hallway is no longer simply an unknown stretch of space. You begin recognizing where a wall might end. Where an
opening begins. Where there's enough room to move."

NO NARRATION — 6 seconds. We enter a striking first-person visualization. Click. A nearby wall becomes visible as a
fleeting outline. Click. A doorway emerges. The protagonist stops, checks their position and continues. Every new pulse
refreshes the visualization.

## Scene 6 — What does an echo actually reveal? (2:33–3:04, testing the ability)

VISUAL. A cinematic training room containing three objects: a large metal panel, an open doorway and a soft
fabric-covered obstacle. Each is demonstrated separately. Use physically motivated sound reflections. The metal panel
returns a strong, distinct reflection. The doorway changes the sound by opening the space beyond. The fabric-covered
obstacle produces a weaker, more muted return.

NARRATOR. "But there's something important to understand. Echolocation isn't like suddenly gaining a second pair of
eyes. You don't see colors, read signs, or recognize someone's face through sound. And different surfaces behave very
differently. Some reflect sound strongly. Others absorb much of it. So even a skilled echolocator has to interpret
incomplete information. The world isn't becoming a perfect picture. It's becoming a collection of useful clues."

NO NARRATION — 6 seconds. The protagonist faces three directions and clicks. Each response sounds different. Show the
differences without spoken explanation.

## Scene 7 — The brain starts changing (3:04–3:32, the neuroscience reveal)

VISUAL. Transition directly from the protagonist's head into a stylized, anatomically informed 3D brain. Electrical
activity flows through auditory-processing networks. Then activity appears in regions normally associated with visual
processing. Camera dives through glowing neural pathways. The screen becomes an enormous network of sound waves, with
spatial relationships taking shape. Avoid implying that the brain literally displays colorful holograms.

NARRATOR. "And here's where things get even more interesting. Your brain isn't a fixed machine. It can adapt through
learning. In brain-imaging research, people trained in echolocation showed increased responses to echoes in regions
normally associated with vision. And this wasn't limited to people who were blind. Sighted participants showed changes
too. That doesn't mean hearing has turned into eyesight. It means your brain can learn new ways of extracting
information from sound."

NO NARRATION — 5 seconds. The neural network pulses with a click. The camera moves through it and transitions back into
the real world.

## Scene 8 — The room that swallows sound (3:32–4:02, unexpected twist)

VISUAL. The protagonist enters a supervised acoustic testing room. At first, the surfaces are ordinary and reflective.
Then the environment transforms. Acoustic foam panels cover the walls and ceiling. The music fades away. They click.
Almost no useful reflection returns. The beautiful acoustic visualization collapses into scattered, fading particles.

NARRATOR. "But what happens when echoes become difficult to hear? Imagine entering a room designed to absorb sound. Your
usual reflections grow weaker. The information you've practiced interpreting becomes harder to use. The walls haven't
moved. The room hasn't changed size. Yet the acoustic clues that helped you understand it are disappearing. And
suddenly, you remember an important truth. Echolocation is useful. But it has limits."

NO NARRATION — 7 seconds. A click. An unusually dry acoustic response. Another click. The protagonist pauses, uses their
cane to confirm the nearby space, and moves cautiously. The absence of a strong echo becomes the dramatic moment.

## Scene 9 — The city becomes a wall of sound (4:02–4:31, escalation)

VISUAL. Cut to an enormous, detailed city street. Buses, bicycles, conversations, footsteps, moving traffic, distant
construction. The audio perspective becomes intensely immersive. At first, a beautiful visualization shows sound sources
arriving from different directions. Then the signals overlap. The geometry becomes difficult to interpret. The
protagonist stops at a safe position away from traffic.

NARRATOR. "Now take that ability outside. A city is completely different from a quiet hallway. Traffic. Conversations.
Footsteps. Engines. Reflections bouncing between buildings. Some of those sounds help you understand what's happening.
Others can mask the quiet echoes you're trying to notice. And this is where real navigation skills, mobility tools, and
experience matter. Echolocation can contribute information. It isn't a replacement for a cane, accessible signals, or
learning how to travel safely."

NO NARRATION — 5 seconds. The protagonist listens. The loudest sounds gradually become distinguishable layers. An
accessible pedestrian signal sounds in the distance. The character continues along the sidewalk.

## Scene 10 — The sound of rain (4:31–5:02, major cinematic spectacle)

VISUAL. Clouds roll over the city. Rain begins. One drop lands on a metallic handrail. Then hundreds strike the
pavement. Slow motion. Water splashes against stone, umbrellas and windows. The camera pulls high above the street,
showing a beautiful storm from an external perspective. Cut back into the protagonist's experience. The acoustic world
becomes denser with sound, but the rain also makes faint clicks harder to distinguish.

NARRATOR. "Then it starts raining. And the entire soundscape changes. Drops strike pavement, metal, leaves, umbrellas,
glass. Thousands of tiny impacts create new sounds across the environment. But rain doesn't simply reveal a perfect map.
It can also drown out quieter echoes. Every environment has its own acoustic character. And learning to navigate with
sound means learning when to trust a clue, when to check it, and when to slow down."

NO NARRATION — 6 seconds. A dramatic overhead tracking shot follows a sheet of rain rolling across the plaza. The
soundtrack becomes purely environmental for several seconds. Then the character steps beneath a covered walkway. The
acoustic atmosphere changes immediately.

## Scene 11 — You don't need a perfect picture (5:02–5:32, changing the perspective)

VISUAL. A friend approaches the protagonist along the covered walkway. We first see the friend from a cinematic
third-person perspective. Then cut to the protagonist's experience. We hear footsteps, breathing, clothing movement and
the friend's voice. The protagonist turns toward the voice. The friend reaches out and speaks. The protagonist smiles.
The image transitions from an abstract acoustic visualization into a realistic wide shot of the two characters standing
together.

NARRATOR. "Here's something easy to forget. Understanding the world isn't only about knowing what things look like. A
familiar voice tells you who's nearby. Footsteps reveal that someone is approaching. Touch gives you details that sound
alone cannot. And memory helps you understand places you've learned. Blind people use many different combinations of
senses, skills and tools. Echolocation is one remarkable possibility among them. The goal isn't to recreate eyesight.
It's to gain useful information and greater independence."

NO NARRATION — 5 seconds. The friend opens a door. The protagonist hears the difference in the room beyond. They enter
together.

## Scene 12 — The final experiment (5:32–6:03, build toward a payoff)

VISUAL. The protagonist returns to a supervised training environment, now several months after the beginning. The space
is a vast, unfamiliar hall. The room contains several large obstacles and two possible passages. A trained instructor
observes. The protagonist carries their mobility cane. The lights dim for the cinematic presentation, although lighting
is irrelevant to the protagonist's lack of sight. A camera sweeps above the environment.

NARRATOR. "Months ago, even finding your way across a bedroom felt unfamiliar. Now, you've learned to recognize patterns
in reflected sound. So let's try one last experiment. A large room. An obstacle ahead. Two possible passages. No visual
information. Just the tools and skills you've practiced. Could you work out where the open route begins?"

NO NARRATION — 9 seconds. Click. The camera follows the wave. A broad reflection suggests the obstacle ahead. Click. One
direction sounds more enclosed. The other changes as the sound enters an open passage. The protagonist checks the route
with their cane and takes the correct path. Music builds with each careful movement.

## Scene 13 — The world returns in a different form (6:03–6:31, emotional and visual climax)

VISUAL. The camera rises above the enormous hall. The building's surfaces dissolve into a beautiful abstract sound-field
visualization. Walls emerge as temporary planes. Passages appear as open regions. Sound travels through the space in
waves. Now the entire environment transitions into an expansive, stylized city visualization. We see roads, buildings,
people, water and open spaces through changing perspectives. These are artistic illustrations of sensory information,
not literal visual experiences.

NARRATOR. "Think about how extraordinary that is. You began by losing one way of understanding the world. But with time,
practice, and the right support, you learned to use information you once ignored. Not every blind person uses
echolocation. And learning it doesn't give anyone supernatural abilities. But for some people, reflected sound can
become a genuinely useful part of navigating space. The world hasn't become visible again. You've simply learned a
different way to understand part of it."

NO NARRATION — 5 seconds. No subtitles. Three measured clicks echo through a vast, beautiful acoustic visualization. Each
click reveals another part of the environment before fading. The music reaches its emotional peak.

## Scene 14 — The last question (6:31–7:00, ending)

VISUAL. Return to the bedroom from the opening. Same bed. Same phone. Same window. But months have passed. The
protagonist confidently places the phone on the bedside table. They walk toward the door, using the space they've
learned. Cut from ordinary third-person cinematography into a restrained abstract sound representation. The door opens.
A warm wash of real-world ambience fills the soundtrack.

NARRATOR. "So, what if you went blind? It wouldn't mean the end of understanding your surroundings. Your life would
change. You'd face real challenges. And no single skill would solve all of them. But echolocation reveals something
fascinating about the human brain. It can learn to find meaning in the smallest signals. And maybe that's the most
incredible part. Sometimes, the world has more information to offer than we ever knew how to notice."

NO NARRATION — FINAL 5 SECONDS. Click. A brief acoustic reflection. The image fades gently into darkness while the sound
and music finish naturally.

END.

## Notes for production (from the screenplay)

- Do not generate continuous narration. All NO NARRATION sections are deliberate.
- The 7-minute duration is a target, not a reason to cut speech or speed up the voice. Time the film from the actual
  narration recordings.
- Use strong but natural sound effects and music that rises during narrator-free sequences.
- All acoustic visualizations are artistic interpretations, not claims that people literally perceive glowing objects.
- Never imply that blindness automatically grants enhanced hearing or that echolocation replaces mobility tools.
- Maintain camera movement, changing compositions, meaningful character acting and visible physical events.
- Build scenes around real actions, not static environments covered by particle effects.
- Preserve the protagonist's design and the film's established environment throughout the episode.
- The final film should feel like one continuous journey with an opening, a discovery, setbacks, progress and resolution.
